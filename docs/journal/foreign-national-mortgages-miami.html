Foreign National Mortgages: How Non-Residents Finance a Miami Home

You don't need a U.S. credit score to finance a Miami home, but you do need the right kind of loan, more paperwork and a plan made before you fly in.

By Vinicius Cadengue, Riley Smith Group at Compass
Nov. 28, 2026 · 4 min read

A few times a month, someone sends me a WhatsApp from São Paulo or Bogotá with some version of the same question: "Vini, I live outside the U.S. and have no American credit. Can a bank here even lend me money?" Usually it comes with a screenshot of a condo they already love.

The short answer is yes, many foreign nationals do finance Miami homes. The longer answer matters more, because the loan you can get as a non-resident works differently from the one your cousin in Orlando got. Knowing that before you fly in saves time, money and at least one awkward phone call with a lender.

Why the usual loans don't fit

Most American mortgages are built to be sold to Fannie Mae or insured by the FHA, and both programs are written for people who live here. Fannie Mae's Selling Guide says it buys loans made to non-U.S. citizens who are lawful permanent or non-permanent residents, on the same terms as citizens. That covers green card holders and many visa holders living in the U.S. It does not describe a buyer whose home, job and taxes are all in another country.

FHA loans got narrower recently. HUD's Mortgagee Letter 2025-09 removed the "non-permanent resident" category from FHA's single-family programs, effective May 25, 2025. So FHA is now for citizens and permanent residents, and it was never meant for someone buying a vacation condo from abroad anyway.

None of this means the door is closed. It means non-residents usually walk through a different one.

What a foreign national loan looks like

Lenders offer what the industry calls foreign national loans. They are usually non-QM, which simply means the loan sits outside the standard government-backed rulebook and each lender writes its own guidelines. NerdWallet describes these loans as typically used by borrowers living outside the U.S. to buy second homes or investment properties. It also notes that foreign nationals tend to pay higher interest rates and face larger minimum down payments than local buyers.

How much higher and how much larger depends on the lender, the property and your profile, and it changes often. I won't quote you a number here because the honest answer is "it depends, and it moves." What I can tell you is that the buyers who have the smoothest time come in expecting a bigger check at closing than an American buyer would write, and they are pleasantly surprised if it ends up smaller.

If the plan is to rent the property out, some investors look at DSCR loans instead. Those lean on the rent the property is expected to bring in rather than on your personal income, and NerdWallet notes that lenders often prefer the borrower to buy through an LLC or corporation. Whether that structure makes sense for you is a question for your accountant and attorney, not your realtor. I just make sure you know it exists.

The paperwork, translated

Here is where clients often get caught off guard. A U.S. lender can't pull your Brazilian or Colombian credit score, so they rebuild your financial picture by hand. Expect to provide your passport and visa, proof of income and employment, bank statements for your accounts, and often a few months of cash reserves on top of the down payment. When there is no U.S. credit history, some lenders accept an international credit report or reference letters from your bank instead. Some borrowers also use an ITIN, an Individual Taxpayer Identification Number, which some loan programs ask for.

Documents in another language usually need translation, and money coming from abroad has to be traceable. If your down payment is sitting in three accounts in two countries, start moving and documenting it early. A wire that lands two days before closing with no paper trail is the kind of surprise nobody enjoys, least of all the underwriter.

The property matters too. Lenders review condo buildings before they lend in them, and some buildings simply don't qualify with some lenders. In Miami, where so many international buyers want condos, I check this early so you don't fall for a unit your lender won't touch.

How I'd plan it

My advice is simple: talk to a lender who does foreign national loans regularly before you start touring, not after. Get a clear sense of the down payment, the reserves and the documents they want, then plug those numbers into my mortgage and monthly cost calculator so the budget includes taxes, insurance and HOA fees, not just the loan payment.

From there we shop with a real number, write offers with realistic timelines, and line up your documents while we look. If you want the bigger picture on taxes and the buying process, my guide to buying in Miami as an international buyer is a good next read, and if you're thinking ahead to selling one day, my piece on FIRPTA for foreign sellers explains what happens at that end.

Financing from abroad takes a little more paperwork and a little more patience. It's still very doable. Most of the stress I see comes from surprises, and most surprises are avoidable if you ask the right questions in the first week instead of the last.

Questions readers ask

Can I get a mortgage in Miami without a U.S. credit score?Many lenders that offer foreign national loans work without a U.S. score. They typically review an international credit report or bank reference letters, along with your income, bank statements and reserves. Requirements vary by lender, so compare a few.

Can a non-resident use an FHA loan?No. FHA loans are for U.S. citizens and lawful permanent residents. HUD removed the non-permanent resident category from FHA's single-family programs effective May 25, 2025, and FHA was never designed for buyers living abroad.

Should I pay cash or finance?It depends on your goals, your tax situation at home and in the U.S., and how much liquidity you want to keep. Many international buyers finance; others prefer cash for speed. Talk it through with a lender and a tax advisor before deciding.
