FIRPTA Explained: What Foreign Sellers Keep at Closing

If you live outside the U.S. and sell a Miami home, the IRS can hold back part of the price at closing. Here is how FIRPTA works and how to plan for it.

By Vinicius Cadengue, Riley Smith Group at Compass
Nov. 22, 2026 · 5 min read

“So if I sell for eight hundred thousand, how much of that actually lands in my account?”

Clients who live outside the United States ask me some version of this question almost every time we sit down to talk about selling. They have run the numbers on price, commission and closing costs. Then someone, usually a friend back home who sold a few years ago, mentions a word they have never heard before: FIRPTA.

FIRPTA is the Foreign Investment in Real Property Tax Act, and it decides how much of a foreign seller’s money stays behind at the closing table. It is not a new tax. It is a withholding rule, a kind of deposit the IRS holds until your actual tax is settled. But if nobody explains it before you list, it can feel like a third of your profit just vanished. So let me explain it now, while you still have time to plan.

What FIRPTA actually takes

When a foreign person sells U.S. real estate, the IRS generally requires the buyer to withhold 15% of the “amount realized” and send it to the IRS. The amount realized is, in plain terms, the sale price: the cash paid, plus the value of any other property exchanged, plus any debt the buyer takes over. That last part matters. FIRPTA is calculated on the price, not on your profit.

Here is what that looks like with simple math. Sell a condo for $800,000, and 15% is $120,000. That $120,000 is held back even if you bought the place for $780,000 and made almost nothing. Your mortgage payoff, the commission and closing costs still come out of the remaining proceeds, so a seller with a large loan can walk away with far less cash on closing day than expected.

Technically, the buyer is the one responsible for withholding, and the IRS can hold a buyer liable if they skip it. In practice, at most closings I have been part of, the title company or closing attorney handles the forms and the payment for the buyer. Either way, it is not optional, and it is not something a seller can negotiate away in the contract.

“FIRPTA is calculated on the price, not on your profit.”

When the number is smaller, or zero

There are exceptions, and two of them come up often in Miami. If the buyer is an individual buying the home to live in, and the price is $300,000 or less, no withholding is required. If the buyer is buying it as a residence and the price is above $300,000 but not more than $1 million, the rate drops to 10%.

The catch is that the residence test belongs to the buyer, not to you. The buyer, or a member of their family, has to have definite plans to live in the property at least half of the days it is used during each of the first two years after the purchase. A buyer purchasing a rental or a vacation unit they will rarely use does not qualify, and the full 15% applies. This is one reason I ask early who is likely to buy a given listing. A starter condo in a neighborhood full of first-time owners is a very different FIRPTA picture from a high-floor unit that investors love.

Getting it back, or holding less

The money withheld is a credit toward whatever U.S. tax you actually owe on the sale. The buyer has 20 days after closing to file IRS Forms 8288 and 8288-A and send in the withholding. The IRS then stamps a copy of Form 8288-A and mails it to you. To get credit, you generally attach that stamped copy to a U.S. income tax return, which for a nonresident individual is usually Form 1040-NR, or to an application for an early refund. If your real tax on the gain is lower than what was withheld, the difference comes back to you.

Here is the detail that trips people up: the IRS will not send you that stamped copy if your taxpayer identification number is missing from the form. For a nonresident who is not eligible for a Social Security number, that means an ITIN. Sellers who wait until after closing to sort this out can wait a long time for their own money.

There is also a way to reduce the withholding before closing. Either the buyer or the seller can ask the IRS for a withholding certificate using Form 8288-B, usually when the expected tax is much lower than 15% of the price. The IRS says it generally acts on these within 90 days of receiving a complete application. The buyer still withholds at closing, but if the application is in by the day of transfer, the money does not have to go to the IRS until 20 days after the IRS mails its decision. Plan for this weeks before you list, not the week of closing.

How I plan a sale with FIRPTA in mind

When an international client tells me they are thinking of selling, I start with the FIRPTA math before we talk about staging or photos. We look at a likely price range, what 15% or 10% would mean, the mortgage payoff and the realistic cash at closing. Then I ask them to bring in a cross-border CPA early, someone who can confirm the actual tax on the gain, get an ITIN started if needed and decide whether a withholding certificate is worth filing.

None of this should scare you off selling. Plenty of foreign owners sell Miami property every year and get back what they are owed. The ones who are surprised are the ones who heard about FIRPTA for the first time from the closing statement. Questions readers ask

Is FIRPTA an extra tax on top of capital gains tax?No. It is a withholding, a prepayment toward the U.S. tax you actually owe on the sale. If the amount withheld is more than your real tax, you can claim the difference back by filing a U.S. tax return with the stamped Form 8288-A.

Does FIRPTA apply if I am a U.S. citizen or green card holder living in Brazil?FIRPTA withholding applies to sales by foreign persons. U.S. citizens and resident aliens generally are not foreign persons for this purpose, and a seller can certify that they are not a foreign person so withholding does not apply. Confirm your status with a CPA before you list.

Can I reduce the 15% before closing?Possibly. You or the buyer can apply to the IRS for a withholding certificate with Form 8288-B, which can lower the amount when your expected tax is smaller. The IRS says it generally acts within 90 days of a complete application, so start well before closing.
