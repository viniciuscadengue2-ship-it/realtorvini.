Why So Many Brazilians Now Call Miami Home

A big, established community, a currency that feels safe and a city that works in Portuguese: what draws Brazilian families and investors to South Florida, and what buyers and sellers should take from it.

By Vinicius Cadengue, Riley Smith Group at Compass
Nov. 26, 2026 · 4 min read

"Vini, everyone we know is either moving to Miami or already here. Are we late?"

I hear some version of that question almost every week, usually over WhatsApp, often in Portuguese, sometimes from a family on its third trip to Florida this year. It is a fair question. Walk through Brickell on a Saturday, order a pão de queijo in Broward, or listen in at a kids' soccer game in Weston, and it can feel like half of São Paulo had the same idea at the same time.

The numbers back up the feeling. And if you are a Brazilian buyer, an investor, or a seller hoping to reach one, understanding why this community keeps choosing South Florida tells you a lot about what will sell, what will rent and what buyers will ask you about first.

A community big enough to feel like home

Start with scale. According to the Migration Policy Institute, about 725,000 Brazilian-born people lived in the United States in 2024, and 23 percent of them lived in Florida over 2020 to 2024, more than in any other state. Broward, Miami-Dade and Palm Beach counties are all among the top five U.S. counties for Brazilian immigrants. Brazil's own foreign ministry counts differently, using consular estimates, and puts roughly 400,000 Brazilians in the jurisdiction of its Miami consulate alone (2023 estimate).

Why does that matter for real estate? People move to where life is already set up for them. A Brazilian family arriving in South Florida can find a church service, a dentist, a lawyer, a butcher and a padaria that all work in Portuguese. That lowers the risk of the move, and it is a big reason so many first trips turn into second homes, and second homes into full relocations.

Buyers, not just visitors

The other half of the story is money. Miami REALTORS' 2025 international report, which covers sales from August 2024 through July 2025, ranks Miami as the number one U.S. market for foreign home buyers. Brazil was essentially tied with Mexico for third place among foreign buyer countries in South Florida, at 7 percent of foreign buyers, behind Colombia and Argentina.

The profile of those Brazilian buyers is interesting. Their median purchase price was $777,400, second only to Mexican buyers in the same report. Sixty-seven percent bought in Miami-Dade, 63 percent bought condos, and 73 percent bought in a central city or urban area. About half paid all cash. And 73 percent planned to use the property as a rental, a vacation home, or both.

For many families, a Miami condo is less a luxury and more a savings account with a balcony.

That last figure is the one I think about most. For many families, a Miami condo is less a luxury and more a savings account with a balcony: a place to stay during school holidays, rented out the rest of the year, and held in dollars.

Why dollars, and why Miami specifically

Miami REALTORS points to two forces behind foreign buying overall in its latest report: shifts in the U.S. dollar and political instability in parts of Latin America, which push buyers to look for safe places to put their money. In the same survey, foreign buyers said security, profitability and location drove their decisions. None of that is unique to Brazil, but Brazilian clients tend to say it out loud. They have lived through currency swings, and owning a real asset priced in dollars feels like insurance.

Then there are the practical reasons. They don't show up in any survey. Miami is only one or two hours behind Brasília, depending on the time of year, so a business owner can run a company back home without working at midnight. The flights are overnight, which means you leave São Paulo after dinner and land in time for breakfast. And the weather, let's be honest, requires very little adjustment. Nobody has ever moved from Recife to Miami to complain about the cold.

Clients often ask me where "the Brazilian neighborhood" is. The real answer is that there isn't just one. Investors who want rental income lean toward Brickell, Downtown and Sunny Isles condos. Families who want space and good schools look at Doral, Weston, Kendall and Coral Gables. You can compare the areas on my neighborhoods page, and if you are coming in December, my article on buying in Miami during the holidays explains how to turn a family trip into a productive house-hunting trip.

What this means if you are buying or selling

If you are a Brazilian buyer, the community's size is an advantage, but it is not a strategy. Decide early whether the property is mostly for you or mostly for rental income, because that one choice changes everything: building rules on short-term rentals, HOA fees, insurance and which neighborhoods make sense. Get your financing and tax picture clear before you fall in love with a view. My guide on buying in Miami as an international buyer covers the process, and if you may sell one day from abroad, read how FIRPTA works at closing now, not later.

If you are a seller, especially of a condo in an urban area, know that a large share of your likely buyers may be reading your listing from Brazil. Good photos, a clear video tour, honest information about the building's finances and an agent who can answer questions in Portuguese at 9pm can make the difference between a showing and a pass.

So, are you late? I don't think so. A community this size didn't happen by accident, and the reasons behind it haven't gone anywhere. Miami still feels familiar, and the dollar still feels safe. The best time to plan is before the next wave of your friends calls you to ask for advice.

Questions readers ask

Where do most Brazilians live in South Florida?There isn't a single Brazilian neighborhood. The Migration Policy Institute lists Broward, Miami-Dade and Palm Beach among the top five U.S. counties for Brazilian immigrants. Investors often look at urban condos in areas like Brickell and Downtown, while families tend to look at suburbs with more space and good schools.

Can a Brazilian buy a home in Miami without living in the U.S.?Yes. Foreign nationals can buy property in Florida, and in the Miami REALTORS 2025 international report, 75% of Brazilian buyers lived abroad. Financing, taxes and how you hold title work differently for non-residents, so talk to a lender, a tax advisor and an attorney before you make an offer.
